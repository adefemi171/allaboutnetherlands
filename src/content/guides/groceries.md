This guide is for anyone who has just moved to the Netherlands and is doing their first grocery shopping. It explains the deposit on bottles and cans, why you pay for a plastic bag, how to compare prices, the rules for alcohol and tobacco, and when shops are open.

## Bring a bag

Shops may not give you a free plastic bag. This has been the rule since 1 January 2016. You can buy one at the till. The shop sets the price. The government gives no minimum or maximum, but recommends €0.25.

Some bags may still be free:

- very thin bags for loose fruit, vegetables and other unpacked food
- bags that stop raw meat, fish or poultry from leaking
- paper bags, cloth bags and jute sacks, unless they have a plastic coating or lining

Bags made of biodegradable or bio-based plastic count as plastic. They are not free either. Bring your own bag to avoid paying.

## Deposit on bottles and cans

When you buy a drink in a plastic bottle or a can, you pay a deposit (*statiegeld*) on top of the price. You get it back when you return the empty bottle or can.

| Packaging | Deposit |
| --- | --- |
| Large plastic bottle of water or soft drink | €0.25 |
| Small plastic bottle of water or soft drink | €0.15 |
| Drinks can | €0.15 |

For plastic juice bottles, a deposit is possible but not required.

You can tell which bottles and cans have a deposit from the deposit logo on the label.

**Where to return them.** Supermarkets larger than 200 m² and staffed petrol stations along the motorway pay out the deposit. Most use a machine, where you put in bottles and cans one by one. Some places have a bulk machine for more than 100 items at once. A small shop may take them back by hand at the till, but may set a maximum and may refuse items you bought elsewhere. You get the money back in cash, on your card, by payment request, or set off against your shopping, depending on the place. The Statiegeld Nederland website has a map of return points (*Locatiewijzer*).

Other places, such as sports clubs, may choose to give the deposit to a charity instead of paying it out. Donation points are not official return points: you do not get your money back there.

**So the machine accepts them:**

- the deposit logo must be on the label
- the barcode must be whole and readable
- keep the bottle or can in its original shape. A small dent is fine, but do not crush it flat
- leaving the cap on is not required, but helps the bottle keep its shape

If the machine refuses an item, try again. If it still does not work, ask a member of staff.

In November 2025 the government said the deposit amounts will not go up. It also announced more return points, faster repairs of broken machines and an app to find the nearest working return point.

## Compare the price per kilo or litre

Every product in a shop must have a price that includes VAT. It can be on the product itself or on the shelf.

For packaged products, the shop must also show the price per unit of measure, for example per kilo, per litre or per metre. For loose products, such as fruit by weight, the shop only has to show the price per unit. This is the law (*Besluit prijsaanduiding producten*). Some products are exempt, and packs that hold different products do not need a unit price.

Compare the unit price, not the price of the pack. Packs come in different sizes, so a lower pack price does not mean the product is cheaper.

## Compare supermarket prices

Compears ([compears.shop](https://compears.shop)) is a website that compares supermarket prices. It is made by Runeward Labs, the same maker as this site.

- It compares prices per litre, per kilogram or per piece, and keeps different units apart. This is the same unit-price idea as above, across shops.
- You can scan a product's barcode.
- For each price it shows the source and the date the price was seen.
- It has offers (*aanbiedingen*), shopping lists and wishes, and shopping guides.
- You can install it on your phone's home screen.

It is live for the Netherlands, the United Kingdom and Germany. In the Dutch version the site is in Dutch, and the menu has an option to switch to English.

## VAT on food

VAT (*btw*) is already in the shelf price. Food and non-alcoholic drinks fall under the low rate of 9%. Beer with more than 0.5% alcohol and other drinks with more than 1.2% alcohol fall under the general rate of 21%.

## Alcohol

Shops may not sell alcohol to anyone under 18. If staff cannot see that you are clearly 18 or older, they must check your age with an ID document, such as your passport or ID card. So carry it when you buy alcohol. The ban also applies when an adult buys alcohol that is clearly meant for someone under 18.

Supermarkets may sell beer and wine, but not spirits. In the law, spirits (*sterke drank*) are drinks of 15% alcohol or more, but wine does not count as spirits. To buy spirits such as whisky, vodka or gin, go to a liquor shop (*slijterij*).

Alcohol in a supermarket is sold closed. You may not open or taste it in the shop.

Shops may not give more than 25% discount on drinks with more than 0.5% alcohol.

## Tobacco

Since 1 July 2024, supermarkets may no longer sell tobacco or related products. For cigarettes you go to a specialist shop, a convenience shop or, until 2030, a petrol station. From 2032, only specialist shops will be allowed to sell tobacco.

## Opening hours

The Shop Opening Hours Act (*Winkeltijdenwet*) sets the basic rules:

- From Monday to Saturday, shops may open between 6:00 and 22:00. Your municipality can allow longer hours.
- On Sundays, shops are closed, unless your municipality allows them to open. Whether supermarkets open on Sunday, and at what times, differs from place to place.
- On these public holidays shops are closed, unless the municipality allows them to open: New Year's Day, Easter Monday, Ascension Day, Whit Monday, Christmas Day and Boxing Day.
- On 4 May (Remembrance of the Dead), shops must close by 19:00 unless the municipality allows longer opening. The same applies to Good Friday and 24 December.

Shops in hospitals and at petrol stations may always open on Sundays.

Check the opening times of your local shop on its website.

## Paying

Most people pay by debit card or phone. In 2025, 83% of payments at the checkout were by card. Cash was used for the other 17%, and it is used less and less.

Shops may choose to accept only debit cards from European customers. So do not count on paying with a credit card. A Dutch debit card is the easiest way to pay. See [Open a Dutch bank account](/guides/bank-account/) for how cards and payments work.

## Food labels

Some packs carry the Nutri-Score: a logo with a letter and a colour that rates how healthy the product's make-up is. It is voluntary, so not every product has one. In the Netherlands, shops and makers may use it officially since 1 January 2024.

Use it to compare similar products, for example two kinds of bread or frozen pizza. A good score does not mean you should eat the product every day. The government's main advice is still to eat as much as possible within the *Schijf van Vijf*, the Dutch healthy eating guide.

## If money is tight

Food banks (*voedselbanken*) give free groceries to people who have too little money left each month for food and other basics. You can sign up on the Voedselbanken Nederland website with your postcode and house number. It shows the food bank near you. You first get four weeks of groceries. After that, you and the food bank decide together whether you can stay.

Food banks cannot help students who live on their own, people without legal residence, people in an asylum seekers' centre (AZC) or people who are homeless.

If you live on a low income, also check whether you can get [healthcare allowance](/guides/health-insurance/).
